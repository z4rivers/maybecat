# Habitat Restoration Language: Raw Material for MaybeCat

Research date: 2026-02-14

The connection to cat advice: EVERY piece of restoration language is also about personal recovery. "The river remembers its original path." "The seed bank was waiting." "It took wolves to change the course of a river." "The first thing that comes back is the sound." These are devastating as oblique answers to "Will I be okay?" or "Should I start over?"

## 1. RESTORATION SCIENCE JARGON (The Poetry Inside Ecology)

These are actual scientific terms that are accidentally beautiful when relocated as oblique life advice.

### Succession
The process of how species compositions change in an ecological community over time. Communities in early succession are dominated by fast-growing, well-dispersed species called **pioneer species** -- organisms that colonize newly exposed or disturbed areas. Gradually, the decomposition of those plants contributes to soil formation, and more and larger plants begin to colonize. The process culminates in a **climax community** -- a stable and diverse ecosystem in equilibrium.
- Source: University of Chicago News, "What is ecological succession?"

### Recruitment
In population dynamics, the process by which new individuals are added to a population. For any species to be considered successful, it must replace itself generation after generation -- a process called recruitment.
- Source: In Defense of Plants, "Let's Talk About Recruitment"

### Seed Bank
Viable seeds present in forest soil and litter. Seed banks are "important buffers that impart resiliency on biological systems." Dormancy provides protection from inhospitable conditions. When lucky, at least some of these individuals will awake in the future when the environment is more conducive for growth and reproduction. From 50 to 90% of wild plant species worldwide produce seeds that are dormant upon maturity. Soil seed banks "store information and impart memory that gives rise to multi-scale structures and networks spanning collections of cells to entire ecosystems."
- Source: Nature Communications, "Principles of seed banks and the emergence of complexity from dormancy" (2021)
- Source: High Country News, "Underground seed banks hold promise for ecological restoration" (2024)

### Nurse Log
A fallen tree that provides a germination site and microhabitat for seedlings. In early stages of succession, early-colonizing plants can increase the survival and reproductive output of other plants by ameliorating disturbance and stressful conditions on nurse logs.
- Source: PMC, "Plant-plant interactions change during succession on nurse logs" (2021)

### Trophic Cascade
When predators at the top of the food chain influence not just their prey, but species several links down the chain. The influence ripples through entire ecosystems.
- Source: ScienceDirect, "The strength of the Yellowstone trophic cascade after wolf reintroduction" (2025)

### Keystone Species
An animal whose activities support its entire habitat, including the other species that it lives alongside.
- Source: World Wildlife Fund

### Ecotone
The junction where two major communities or biomes meet and blend. Coined in 1904, formed as a combination of ecology + -tone, from the Greek *tonos* or tension -- in other words, **a place where ecologies are in tension.**
- Source: Frederic E. Clements (1904), via Wikipedia

### Edge Effect
The phenomenon of increased variety of plants and animals at the community junction. As edge effects increase, the boundary habitat allows for greater biodiversity.
- Source: Britannica, "Edge effect"

### Legacy Effects
Impacts of a species on abiotic or biotic features of ecosystems that persist for a long time after the species has been extirpated or ceased activity. Land use creates legacy effects on biotic communities that may persist for decades or centuries, even after the landscape has been abandoned and seemingly restored. Scientists refer to these as "the ghosts of ecosystem engineers."
- Source: Biological Theory, "Legacy Effects: The Persistent Impact of Ecological Interactions" (2012)
- Source: Functional Ecology, "The ghosts of ecosystem engineers" (2024)

### Ecological Memory
When a community or ecosystem is lost, some of its properties may remain, leaving behind an ecological memory. The soil properties, spores, seeds, stem fragments, mycorrhizae, species, populations and other remnants of the previous inhabitants contribute to shaping the replacement community and building a new ecosystem. Soil seed banks serve as "an indicator of a restored area's accumulation of biological memory and recovery capacity over time."
- Source: Urban Ecosystems, "Remembering our roots" (2010)

### Rewilding
"Perhaps more a philosophy than a methodological process." The principles: connecting, restoring, and ultimately **letting go.** "Don't just do something, stand there." The philosophy implies a nature-led journey -- one built around natural processes, some surprises and a reasonable lack of expectation.
- Source: Restore Nature, "Rewilding: definitions, philosophy and letting go"

### Other Accidentally Beautiful Terms
- **Pioneer species** -- the first to arrive after devastation
- **Climax community** -- the stable endpoint of recovery
- **Biological memory** -- what the land remembers
- **Recruitment** -- when new life shows up
- **Succession** -- stages of coming back
- **Nurse log** -- what falls becomes what feeds
- **Edge effect** -- life is richest at the borders
- **Ecotone** -- a place where ecologies are in tension
- **Legacy effects** -- the persistent ghosts of what was
- **Seed bank** -- the dormant potential waiting underground

## 2. BEFORE/AFTER TRANSFORMATION DESCRIPTIONS

### Elwha River, Washington -- The River That Came Back

**The dams:** Two dams blocked salmon from 90% of their habitat for nearly a century. 20 million tons of sediment accumulated behind them.

**The removal (2011-2014):** "The freed river was an aqueous concoction of trees, limbs, root wads and likely billions of small sticks, leaves and pine needles." More than 20 million tons of sediment were released -- "several decades' worth of accumulation -- which significantly altered the river channel and floodplain."
- Source: Wild Steelheaders United, "The Elwha River: A wild ride through a decade of dam removal"

**The sediment rebuilds the delta:** "The river had taken a substantial chunk of flesh out of the reservoir's sediments, moving them down and out to the ocean, where it formed a new, large beach and spit."
- Source: USGS Geonarrative, Elwha River Restoration

**Salmon return:** "Within months, eight anadromous species including chinook, coho, pink, and sockeye salmon, plus steelhead trout, swiftly ascended upstream into areas that had been blocked for nearly a century. Fish that had lost 90 percent of their habitat suddenly had access to the entire river corridor again."
- Source: NOAA Fisheries, "The Elwha River Restoration"

**Tribal ceremony (October 2023):** The Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe opened a ceremonial and subsistence salmon fishery for the first time since dam removal. "They celebrated the opening amidst traditional drumming and dancing, ceremonies to bless the salmon and the water." A tribal member said: "It's our medicine. Being able to share that fish on to our family tables again, that is something that is very humbling to elders as well as the tribal community." Families brought children who fished for salmon on the Elwha for the very first time in their lives.
- Source: KNKX Public Radio; Northwest Treaty Tribes; Columbia Climate School

### Yellowstone Wolves -- The Cascade That Changed Rivers

**Before wolves:** Elk browsed freely in river valleys, eating willows, aspens, and cottonwoods to the ground. Riverbanks crumbled. Channels widened. The landscape was stripped.

**After wolves returned (1995):** "Elk that once leisurely browsed in river valleys began avoiding these vulnerable areas. They spent less time in any one spot, always watching for wolves. This behavioral shift gave riverside vegetation -- willows, aspens, cottonwoods -- room to breathe and grow for the first time in decades."
- Source: Rewilding Academy, "How Wolves Change Rivers"

**The cascade:** A 20-year study (2001-2020) revealed a ~1,500% increase in average willow crown volume. "Riverbanks that had been crumbling began to firm up, channels that had widened started to narrow and deepen, water quality improved as sediment loads decreased." When willows recovered, beavers came back. With fewer coyotes hunting small rodents, raptors made a comeback.
- Source: ScienceDirect, "The strength of the Yellowstone trophic cascade" (2025)

### Kissimmee River, Florida -- Un-Channelizing a River

**Before:** The Kissimmee River historically meandered 103 miles through a diversely rich 1-3 mile wide floodplain. From 1962 to 1970, the Army Corps of Engineers dredged a straight canal, shortening it to 56 miles. "40,000 acres of floodplain below Lake Kissimmee dried out, reducing the quality of waterfowl habitat by ninety percent, and the number of herons, egrets and wood storks by two-thirds."
- Source: Florida Atlantic University, Center for Environmental Studies

**After (completed 2021):** "When flooding began again, muck and smothering aquatic weeds were flushed out. Sandbars reemerged. Encroaching dry land trees began dying back. Flooding and continuous flow increased levels of dissolved oxygen in the water, creating near perfect conditions for aquatic invertebrates such as insects, mollusks, worms, crayfish and freshwater shrimp. This, in turn, boosted fish populations and it led to a rise in bird and alligator populations." Species that disappeared during the canal days -- including ibis, bitterns, avocets, and sandpipers -- are back.
- Source: National Geographic, "The Kissimmee: biggest river restoration ever completed"
- Source: World Economic Forum, "How the US's dying Kissimmee River regained its biodiversity"

### Knepp Estate, UK -- Rewilding a Failed Farm

**Before:** A depleted, polluted, dysfunctional farmland on heavy clay that refused attempts at agriculture. By 2000, facing bankruptcy.

**The leap:** "Fences came down and free-roaming animals were introduced. Within months, Nature began to recover."

**After:** "We have gone from a depleted, polluted, dysfunctional farmland to one of the most significant biodiversity hotspots in the UK." -- Isabella Tree. A 900% increase in breeding birds. Nightingale numbers increased from 9 in 1999 to 62 in 2025. "Nightingales arrived in such numbers that we began to have nightingale dinners, taking friends out at night to listen to their astonishing arias." Turtle doves, purple emperor butterflies, peregrine falcons, lesser spotted woodpeckers -- all now breeding. "On a good July day, I can count ten species of butterfly from my desk."
- Source: Isabella Tree, *Wilding* (2018); Country Life interview; Rewilding Britain case study

### Chesapeake Bay Oyster Reefs

**Before:** Due to disease, overfishing, degraded water quality -- only about 1% of the native oyster population remains.

**After restoration:** More than 2,400 acres of healthy reef restored. "An adult oyster can filter up to 50 gallons of water per day." More restored reefs means more filter feeding -- which can result in clearer water near those reefs. Restored reefs remove about seven times as much nitrogen each day as unrestored sand/mud bottom.
- Source: NOAA Fisheries, "Oyster Reef Restoration in the Chesapeake Bay"

## 5. THE ACTIONS OF RECOVERY (What Happens When Ecosystems Come Back)

### Salmon returning to rivers after dam removal
"Within months, eight anadromous species swiftly ascended upstream into areas that had been blocked for nearly a century."
- Source: NOAA Fisheries

### Birds appearing where there were none
Kissimmee River: "Species that disappeared during the days of the canal -- including ibis, bitterns, avocets, and sandpipers -- are back."
- Source: National Geographic

Knepp Estate: Nightingales went from 9 to 62. Turtle doves from 2 to 22.
- Source: Rewilding Britain

### The sound returning to a forest
"Soundscapes can reveal details of environmental destruction or environmental recovery in profound ways -- providing us with details about an ecosystem and its interactions that we cannot really understand through other means."
- Source: earth.fm, "Rewilding and restoration success stories revealed through sound"

### Beavers rebuilding
"The beaver can with time transform agricultural land into a comparatively species-rich and heterogeneous wetland environment." After 12 years of beaver presence, mean plant species richness increased on average by 46% per plot, and cumulative species recorded increased on average by 148%. "Dams slowed water flow, allowing nutrient-rich sediments to settle. Water tables rose. Flooding became less severe, as dams spread water across the landscape."
- Source: Royal Society Publishing, "Rewilding wetlands: beaver as agents of within-habitat heterogeneity" (2018)
- Source: WWF, "Beavers Build Resilience"

### Wolves changing rivers (the Yellowstone cascade)
"Elk that once leisurely browsed in river valleys began avoiding these vulnerable areas... This behavioral shift gave riverside vegetation -- willows, aspens, cottonwoods -- room to breathe and grow for the first time in decades." 1,500% increase in willow crown volume over 20 years. When willows recovered, beavers came back. When beavers came back, the entire hydrology changed.
- Source: ScienceDirect / Rewilding Academy

### Oysters filtering water clear
"An adult oyster can filter up to 50 gallons of water per day." Restored reefs remove seven times as much nitrogen as unrestored bottom.
- Source: NOAA Fisheries

### The first wildflowers in restored prairie
"Pioneering species like annual sunflowers appear, with many growing over 12 feet tall in the initial season." After prescribed burns, "plants grow back quickly, and they produce wildflowers with little effort and no water even during summer droughts."
- Source: Missouri Conservation Heritage Foundation; Iowa State Extension

### Mangroves coming back on their own
"Mother Nature can do such a great job of repairing herself if you give her the opportunity." Damaged mangrove areas may repair themselves through natural processes of secondary succession without physical planting, provided their hydrology functions normally.
- Source: Smithsonian Ocean, "Mangrove Restoration: Letting Mother Nature Do The Work"

### The Kissimmee transformation sequence
"When flooding began again, muck and smothering aquatic weeds were flushed out. Sandbars reemerged. Encroaching dry land trees began dying back. Dissolved oxygen increased. Invertebrates exploded. Fish followed. Then birds. Then alligators."
- Source: National Geographic (paraphrased sequence from reporting)

## 6. LOST/RECOVERED LANDSCAPE WORDS (Robert Macfarlane's Glossaries)

From *Landmarks* (2015) and *The Lost Words* (2017):

### From *Landmarks* glossaries (2,000+ terms from 30+ dialects):

**Ammil** (Devon): The sparkle of morning sunlight through hoar-frost. A beautifully exact word for a fugitive phenomenon.

**Smeuse** (Sussex): The gap in the base of a hedge made by the regular passage of a small animal.

**Zwer** (dialect): The whizzing noise made by a covey of partridges as they break suddenly from cover.

**Pirr** (Shetlandic): A light breath of wind that ruffles the surface of the water.

**Rionnach maoim** (Hebridean Gaelic): The shadows cast by cumulus cloud on moorland on a sunny, windy day.

**Caochan** (Gaelic): A slender moor-stream obscured by vegetation such that it is virtually hidden from sight.

**Feith** (Gaelic): A fine vein-like watercourse running through peat, often dry in the summer.

**Shivelight** (Gerard Manley Hopkins): The lances of sunshine that pierce the canopy of a wood.

**Ungive** (Northamptonshire): To thaw. "The beauty of this variant has to do with the paradox of thaw figured as restraint or retention, and the wintry notion that cold, frost and snow might themselves be a form of gift -- an addition to the landscape that will in time be subtracted by warmth."

**Holloway** (from Anglo-Saxon *Hola Weg*): A sunken path. Ancient desire paths that over centuries the effects of wind, erosion and time itself have sunk into the earth.

**Dimpsey** (West Country): The semi-dark around dawn and dusk.

**Desire path**: A shortcut worn into the landscape by repeated human or animal passage -- a path made by wanting, not by planning.

### Macfarlane's thesis:
"Words are grained into our landscapes, and landscapes are grained into our words." When words are lost, we can't describe our environment, and our connection to it is lessened. "We love the things we name."
- Source: Robert Macfarlane, *Landmarks*

### From *The Lost Words* (2017):

The words removed from the Oxford Junior Dictionary: **acorn, adder, bluebell, bramble, conker, cowslip, cygnet, dandelion, fern, hazel, heather, heron, ivy, kingfisher, lark, mistletoe, moss, newt, otter, pasture, willow** -- replaced by broadband, cut-and-paste, database, chatroom.

Macfarlane and Jackie Morris wrote "spell-poems" as acrostics to summon each word back: "a catalogue and spelling book where the lost words in question comprise vocabulary about flora and fauna." Each word occupies three lavishly illustrated spreads: a poetic summoning spell, a wordless visual eulogy for its vanishment, and a typographic botany of letters spelling it "back into language, hearts, minds and landscape."
- Source: The Marginalian; Cambridge University; CBC Radio

- Source: "Thinking Like a Mountain," *A Sand County Almanac*

## POTENTIAL MAYBECAT RESPONSE SEEDS

These are NOT finished responses -- they are raw seeds showing how restoration language maps to life advice. The actual response writing would follow the cat voice guide.

**From restoration jargon:**
- "The seed bank remembers." (Will I be okay?)
- "Succession takes time. Pioneer species come first." (Should I start over?)
- "You're in an ecotone -- a place where ecologies are in tension." (Am I in the right place?)
- "Legacy effects. The ghosts of what was are still shaping what is." (Why can't I move on?)
- "Recruitment happens. New life just... shows up." (Will I find someone?)
- "Nurse logs. What falls feeds what grows." (Was the failure worth it?)
- "Edge effects. Life is richest at the borders." (I don't fit anywhere.)
- "The climax community takes decades." (When will things stabilize?)

**From transformation stories:**
- "It took wolves to change the course of a river." (Can one person make a difference?)
- "The river remembers its original path." (Can I go back to who I was?)
- "The salmon returned within months." (How long will this take?)
- "Nightingales arrived in such numbers they had dinners to listen." (Will it get better?)
- "Sandbars reemerged. Ibis came back." (Is there hope?)
- "She filtered 50 gallons a day and nobody noticed." (Does my work matter?)
- "Fences came down. Nature did the rest." (Should I stop trying so hard?)

- "I had been my whole life a bell and never knew it." (Am I enough?)
- "The land is like poetry: inexplicably coherent." (Does any of this make sense?)
- "A fierce green fire dying in her eyes." (Should I walk away?)
- "I rest in the grace of the world, and am free." (Will the anxiety stop?)
- "All flourishing is mutual." (Is this relationship worth it?)

**From patience language:**
- "Prairies look underdeveloped for five years before they bloom." (Why isn't anything happening?)
- "The seed bank was waiting." (Should I give up?)
- "Don't just do something. Stand there." (What should I do?)
- "Plants that were pushed out never forgot their home." (Should I go back?)
- "It took ecological time. Not your kind of time." (Why is this so slow?)
