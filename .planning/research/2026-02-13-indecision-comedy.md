## 1. FAMOUS INDECISION MOMENTS

### 1a. George W. Bush -- "Fool Me Once" (Sep 17, 2002)

> "There's an old saying in Tennessee -- I know it's in Texas, probably in Tennessee -- that says, fool me once, shame on -- shame on you. Fool me -- you can't get fooled again."

**Source:** White House archives, remarks at Nashville education event. The correct proverb is "Fool me once, shame on you. Fool me twice, shame on me." Bush reportedly realized mid-sentence that completing the proverb would create a soundbite of a president saying "shame on me," so he bailed -- and created a worse one.

**Why it's gold:** The man chose NEITHER option. He stood at the fork in the road and built a third road out of panic.

### 1b. John Kerry -- The $87 Billion (March 16, 2004)

> "I actually did vote for the $87 billion, before I voted against it."

**Source:** CNN, remarks at Marshall University, Huntington, West Virginia. Kerry later called it "one of those inarticulate moments." He meant he voted for an alternative version (funded by tax increases on wealthy) and against the final version, but the damage was done.

**Why it's gold:** The purest accidental confession of indecision in political history. Both positions, one sentence.

### 1c. Lincoln to McClellan -- "Borrow the Army"

> "My Dear McClellan, if you don't want to use the army I should like to borrow it for a while. Yours respectfully, A. Lincoln."

**Source:** Widely attributed, exact document unconfirmed by historians. Written approx. Oct/Nov 1862 before McClellan's removal. McClellan spent months refusing to advance the Army of the Potomac, despite Lincoln's repeated urging. After the Battle of Antietam, Lee retreated to Virginia and McClellan refused to pursue for six weeks.

**UNVERIFIED** as to exact document, but the Lincoln-McClellan correspondence is extensively documented and this is consistent with Lincoln's known frustrations. Mark as paraphrase.

**Why it's gold:** History's greatest passive-aggressive note. McClellan had the most powerful army on the continent and his strategy was "wait." Lincoln's frustration became the purest expression of watching someone refuse to use what they have.

### 1d. Romney's Etch A Sketch (March 21, 2012)

Santorum: "Whoever you're going to vote for is going to be a completely new candidate."

**Source:** NPR, CNN coverage of 2012 GOP primary.

**Why it's gold:** The campaign accidentally admitted that convictions are erasable. Shake it and start over. Your principles are drawn in magnetic dust.

> "Read my lips: no new taxes."

Said at 1988 Republican National Convention. Raised taxes in 1990. Lost re-election in 1992. The most famous broken promise in modern politics -- not indecision exactly, but the quintessential moment where a man's past self and present self publicly disagreed.

**Source:** 1988 RNC acceptance speech, widely documented.

### 1f. Boris Johnson -- Wrote Both Columns

Boris Johnson reportedly wrote two newspaper columns before the Brexit referendum -- one arguing for Leave, one for Remain -- and chose which to publish. He literally drafted both futures and then picked one.

**Source:** Multiple UK press reports. Tim Shipman, *All Out War* (2016) documents Johnson's wavering.

**Why it's gold:** Most people agonize over decisions. This man COMPLETED both options and THEN couldn't decide.

## 2. LITERARY & PHILOSOPHICAL INDECISION

### 2a. Sylvia Plath -- The Fig Tree (The Bell Jar, 1963)

> "I saw my life branching out before me like the green fig tree in the story. From the tip of every branch, like a fat purple fig, a wonderful future beckoned and winked. One fig was a husband and a happy home and children, and another fig was a famous poet and another fig was a brilliant professor... I saw myself sitting in the crotch of the fig-tree, starving to death, just because I couldn't make up my mind which of the figs I would choose. I wanted each and every one of them, but choosing one meant losing all the rest, and, as I sat there, unable to decide, the figs began to wrinkle and go black, and, one by one, they plopped to the ground at my feet."

**Source:** Sylvia Plath, *The Bell Jar* (1963), Chapter 7. Semi-autobiographical novel.

**Why it's gold:** The single greatest description of choice paralysis in the English language. Every fig is a life unlived. They ROT while she watches. This is what 2 AM feels like when you're 25.

### 2b. Kierkegaard -- The Either/Or Passage (1843)

> "If you marry, you will regret it; if you do not marry, you will also regret it; if you marry or do not marry, you will regret both. Laugh at the world's follies, you will regret it, weep over them, you will also regret that... Hang yourself, you will regret it; do not hang yourself, and you will also regret that; hang yourself or do not hang yourself, you will regret both; whether you hang yourself or do not hang yourself, you will regret both. This, gentlemen, is the sum and substance of all philosophy."

**Source:** Soren Kierkegaard, *Either/Or: A Fragment of Life* (1843).

**Why it's gold:** 180 years old and still the most honest thing ever written about decisions. Every option leads to regret. He proved it with ALGEBRA. "The sum and substance of all philosophy" is: you're screwed either way.

---

### 2c. Kierkegaard -- "Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom"

> "Anxiety is the dizziness of freedom."

**Source:** Soren Kierkegaard, *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844).

**Why it's gold:** Six words. The entire paradox of choice explained. Freedom doesn't feel free. It feels like vertigo.

### 2d. Hamlet -- The Indecision Soliloquy (Act 3, Scene 1)

> "To be, or not to be, that is the question --
> Whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer
> The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune,
> Or to take arms against a sea of troubles,
> And by opposing end them?"

And later in the same speech:

> "Thus conscience does make cowards of us all,
> And thus the native hue of resolution
> Is sicklied o'er with the pale cast of thought,
> And enterprises of great pitch and moment
> With this regard their currents turn awry,
> And lose the name of action."

**Source:** William Shakespeare, *Hamlet*, Act 3, Scene 1 (c. 1600).

**Why it's gold:** "The native hue of resolution / Is sicklied o'er with the pale cast of thought" -- Shakespeare literally says THINKING TOO MUCH makes your willpower go pale and sick. The greatest writer in English diagnosed overthinking as a disease 400 years ago. "And lose the name of action" -- your plans get so overthought they forget they were supposed to DO something.

### 2e. T.S. Eliot -- Prufrock's Peach (1915)

> "Do I dare / Disturb the universe? / In a minute there is time / For decisions and revisions which a minute will reverse."

And:

> "Shall I part my hair behind? Do I dare to eat a peach?"

And:

> "I have measured out my life with coffee spoons."

**Source:** T.S. Eliot, "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" (1915). Published in *Poetry* magazine. Eliot wrote it at age 23.

**Why it's gold:** The man cannot commit to eating a PEACH. He earlier asks if he dares "disturb the universe" -- same energy. The gap between peach and universe is the whole poem. And "measured out my life with coffee spoons" -- existence reduced to the smallest possible increments of commitment.

### 2f. Buridan's Ass -- The Philosophical Paradox

A donkey, equally hungry and thirsty, is placed precisely midway between a stack of hay and a pail of water. Being perfectly rational and unable to prefer one over the other, it dies of both hunger and thirst.

The common variant: two identical piles of hay. The donkey starves to death between them.

**Source:** Named after 14th-century philosopher Jean Buridan, though the paradox predates him. Aristotle used a similar example (a man equally hungry and thirsty). Al-Ghazali used a man choosing between equally good dates.

**Why it's gold:** A donkey dies because it can't pick a haystack. Perfect rationality = perfect paralysis. The universe's cruelest math problem. Also: the paradox is now used in ELECTRICAL ENGINEERING -- digital circuits face the same problem when an input voltage is exactly between 0 and 1.

---

### 2g. Dostoyevsky -- The Underground Man (1864)

> "It was not only that I could not become spiteful, I did not know how to become anything; neither spiteful nor kind, neither a rascal nor an honest man, neither a hero nor an insect."

And:

> "I tell you solemnly, that I have many times tried to become an insect. But I was not equal even to that. I swear, gentlemen, that to be too conscious is an illness -- a real thorough-going illness."

**Source:** Fyodor Dostoyevsky, *Notes from Underground* (1864), Part 1.

**Why it's gold:** He can't even commit to being INSIGNIFICANT. Too much self-awareness is literally diagnosed as a disease. Overthinking as pathology. "I have many times tried to become an insect" is the 19th-century version of "I've been trying to not care but I keep caring about not caring."

### 2h. Camus -- "The Only Serious Philosophical Problem"

> "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem and that is suicide."

Often paraphrased as: "Should I kill myself or have a cup of coffee?"

**Source:** Albert Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942). The coffee paraphrase is NOT a direct Camus quote -- it's a popular restatement. Mark the coffee version as **UNVERIFIED/paraphrase**.

**Why it's gold:** The paraphrase is better than the original. It puts the biggest question and the smallest question at the same table. That IS what 2 AM feels like. Coffee always wins, and that IS the philosophy.

---

### 2i. Sartre -- "Condemned to Be Free" (1946)

> "Man is condemned to be free; because once thrown into the world, he is responsible for everything he does."

**Source:** Jean-Paul Sartre, *Existentialism is a Humanism* (1946 lecture, later published). Also in *Being and Nothingness* (1943).

**Why it's gold:** "Condemned to be free." Freedom as a SENTENCE, not a gift. Every decision is your fault forever. You can't even blame fate.

---

### 2j. Robert Frost -- "The Road Not Taken" (1916) -- The Great Misreading

> "Two roads diverged in a wood, and I -- / I took the one less traveled by, / And that has made all the difference."

BUT earlier in the poem:

> "Though as for that the passing there / Had worn them really about the same."

**Source:** Robert Frost, "The Road Not Taken" (1916). Frost wrote it as a joke about his chronically indecisive friend, poet Edward Thomas, who always lamented they should have taken the other path on walks. Frost once warned an audience: "You have to be careful of that one; it's a tricky poem -- very tricky."

**Why it's gold:** The most misquoted poem in English. Everyone thinks it's about brave individual choices. It's actually about how we invent a narrative AFTER the fact to pretend our random choice was meaningful. The two roads were the same. We just can't live with that. Frost wrote about indecision and people turned it into a motivational poster. He proved his own point.

### 2k. Woody Allen -- "My Speech to the Graduates" (1980)

> "More than any other time in history, mankind faces a crossroads. One path leads to despair and utter hopelessness. The other, to total extinction. Let us pray we have the wisdom to choose correctly."

**Source:** Woody Allen, *Side Effects* (1980), essay "My Speech to the Graduates."

**Why it's gold:** Both options are terrible. The prayer for "wisdom to choose correctly" is the kicker -- as if one of those is the RIGHT answer.

## 3. EVERYDAY INDECISION COMEDY

### 3a. The Sidewalk Shuffle

Two people approaching from opposite directions both step aside -- in the same direction. Then both correct -- in the same direction. Repeat until someone physically stops or one of you dies of embarrassment.

Known as: the sidewalk shuffle, the sidewalk salsa, the footpath foxtrot, the pavement polka.

**Source:** Named by internet culture; studied by University of Melbourne researchers. It happens because human mirror neurons cause us to unconsciously match the other person's movements. We literally cannot stop being polite.

**Why it's gold:** Two perfectly well-meaning people locked in an infinite loop of mutual accommodation. Politeness as paralysis. The body's version of "no you go first" -- "no YOU go first."

---

### 3b. The Cheesecake Factory Menu

250 menu items. 85 different ways to order chicken. 50 types of cheesecake. Average time to decide: 15+ minutes. The menu is 21 pages long.

Twitter user Max Krieger described it as a "postmodern design hellscape." Kate McKinnon in *The Spy Who Dumped Me*: "God, that menu. Too many options."

**Source:** Time magazine viral rant coverage, 2017. Menu data from Cheesecake Factory corporate/press materials.

**Why it's gold:** The menu is a physical manifestation of the paradox of choice. You came here to eat. You will leave having read a novel and ordered the chicken.

---

### 3c. The "I Don't Care, You Pick" Loop

The infinite restaurant decision loop between couples:
- "Where do you want to eat?" / "I don't care, you pick."
- "How about Italian?" / "No, not Italian."
- "Chinese?" / "We just had Chinese."
- "Then YOU pick." / "I said I don't care!"

**Source:** Universal human experience. Documented in relationship humor across all platforms. One proposed solution: "Guess where I'm taking you!" -- the first thing they guess is where you go.

**Why it's gold:** "I don't care" means "I care deeply but will not reveal which option I've already decided on, and you must guess correctly or face consequences."

---

### 3d. Netflix Paralysis

Netflix users spend an average of 18 minutes deciding what to watch. Netflix's own data shows users browse 60-90 seconds before giving up entirely. 21% of streaming subscribers report simply giving up and watching nothing if they can't decide. Browsing time has increased from 7 minutes to 10.5 minutes in just two years.

**Source:** Reelgood/Learndipity study (2016), Nielsen streaming report (2019), Netflix internal data via Quartz.

**Why it's gold:** We have access to every movie and show ever made and we choose NOTHING. We spent more time deciding than the movie would have lasted. 21% of people with infinite options pick zero.

---

### 3e. Menu Anxiety (Gen Z Edition)

86% of Gen Z adults experience "menu anxiety" when ordering at restaurants. 34% of young adults delegate ordering to someone else at the table. Some report pre-researching menus the night before so they don't have to decide on the spot.

**Source:** Prezzo restaurant chain study (UK), parallel US studies, 2023. Published in Food Institute, Psychiatrist.com.

**Why it's gold:** An entire generation is outsourcing restaurant decisions to friends. They do RECONNAISSANCE on menus. Like it's a military operation. "What's the soup?" has become a high-stakes question.

---

### 3f. The Jam Study -- Decision Paralysis Proven in a Supermarket

Researchers set up a jam tasting booth. One day: 6 jams. Another day: 24 jams. The 24-jam display attracted more browsers. But the 6-jam display produced TEN TIMES more purchases (30% vs. 3%).

**Source:** Dr. Sheena Iyengar (Columbia) and Mark Lepper (Stanford). Popularized by Barry Schwartz in *The Paradox of Choice* (2004). Note: subsequent meta-analysis (2010) found mixed replication results -- the effect may be more context-dependent than originally claimed.

**Why it's gold:** When given 24 options, 97% of people chose NOTHING. More choice = more paralysis. We'd rather have no jam than pick the wrong jam.

### 3g. Barry Schwartz's Jeans Shopping

Barry Schwartz went to buy jeans. Asked for 32 waist, 28 leg. The salesperson asked: Stone washed? Acid washed? Torn? Distressed? Zip fly or button fly? Faded or regular? Slim fit, easy fit, or relaxed fit? Straight leg, skinny leg, or extra baggy?

**Source:** Barry Schwartz, *The Paradox of Choice* (2004), opening anecdote.

**Why it's gold:** The man wanted PANTS. He got an existential crisis.

### 3i. The Shopping Cart Graveyard

70% of online shopping carts are abandoned before checkout. People fill the cart, get to the final step, and close the tab. Digital indecision at industrial scale.

**Source:** Baymard Institute meta-analysis (multiple studies).

**Why it's gold:** Billions of decisions made and then unmade at the last second. The "actually, never mind" economy.

Typing a message, deleting it, retyping it, deleting it, retyping a shorter version, deleting it, sending "hey" -- the digital Hamlet soliloquy. The person on the other end sees "typing..." appear and disappear five times.

**Source:** Universal experience. The "typing indicator" is the modern equivalent of watching someone's face change as they decide what to say.

## 5. CULTURAL EXPRESSIONS FOR INDECISION

### 5a. German: "Die Qual der Wahl"

Literally: "the torture of choice." Used when you have too many good options and can't pick. Origin: German translation of an 18th-century French comedy title, *L'embarrass des richesses*.

Related proverb: **"Wer die Wahl hat, hat die Qual"** -- "Whoever has the choice, has the torment."

**Why it's gold:** German has a word for the PAIN of having options. They diagnosed it as torture. Leave it to Germans to name the suffering with precision.

---

### 5b. Greek: "Akrasia"

Literally: "without power" or "lack of self-control." Acting against your own better judgment. You know the right choice. You make the wrong one. On purpose.

Aristotle dedicated Book VII of the *Nicomachean Ethics* to it. Socrates said it was impossible (you can't know the right thing and do the wrong thing). Aristotle said: yes you can, watch me.

**Why it's gold:** The Greeks had a WORD for the thing where you know what to do and then don't do it. This isn't indecision. This is decision-defiance. Your brain said go left. Your body went right. Ancient Greek for "I KNOW but..."

---

### 5c. Japanese: "Tsundoku"

Buying books and letting them pile up without reading them. From *tsunde-oku* (to let things pile up) + *dokusho* (reading).

**Source:** Originated in Meiji era (1868-1912) as satire of teachers who owned books but didn't read them.

**Why it's gold:** The inability to commit to reading what you chose to buy. Every book is a decision you made and then walked away from. Your bookshelf is a graveyard of abandoned intentions.

---

### 5d. Italian: "Dolce Far Niente"

"The sweetness of doing nothing." Not laziness -- the active enjoyment of not choosing, not doing, not deciding.

**Why it's gold:** The Italians didn't just accept inaction -- they CELEBRATED it. They gave it a name that sounds like a dessert. While everyone else agonizes over decisions, Italy invented the philosophy of "none of the above, and I feel GREAT about it."

---

### 5e. Spanish: "Manana"

Literally "tomorrow." Culturally: the flexible, noncommittal relationship with when things happen. Not procrastination -- a different philosophical framework for time and urgency entirely.

**Why it's gold:** A culture that turned "later" into a lifestyle. Not indecision -- decided postponement with zero guilt.

---

### 5f. English Idioms for Indecision

- **Shilly-shally** -- hesitating endlessly (from "Shall I? Shall I?" -- literally asking yourself the same question twice)
- **On the fence** -- can't pick a side (origin: 19th century, arbiters who sat on fences between neighbors to hear both sides)
- **Hem and haw** -- the sounds you make instead of answering
- **Of two minds** -- you have TWO minds and they disagree
- **Wishy-washy** -- from "wash" -- you've been rinsed of all conviction
- **Blowing hot and cold** -- from Aesop's fable of the man who blew on his hands to warm them and on his soup to cool it
- **Dithering** -- moving without direction, like a ship without wind

---

### 5g. Proverbs About Indecision

- **"He who hesitates is lost."** -- English proverb, from Joseph Addison's play *Cato* (1713)
- **"Between two stools, one falls to the ground."** -- Medieval Latin/French proverb. Can't sit in two chairs = you hit the floor.
- **"A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush."** -- Choose what you have over what you might get.
- **"When you chase two rabbits, you catch neither."** -- Russian proverb.

## 6. PHILOSOPHY OF CHOICE & MODERN PSYCHOLOGY

### 6a. Dan Gilbert -- Synthetic Happiness (TED, 2004)

> "Freedom -- the ability to make up your mind and change your mind -- is the friend of natural happiness... But freedom to choose, to change and make up your mind, is the enemy of synthetic happiness."

Our "psychological immune system" makes us happy with what we got -- BUT only if we can't change it. Reversible decisions make us LESS happy. The ability to change your mind = the inability to accept your choice.

**Source:** Dan Gilbert, "The Surprising Science of Happiness" TED Talk (2004). Book: *Stumbling on Happiness*.

**Why it's gold:** MORE options = LESS happiness. Commitment makes you happy. Keeping your options open makes you miserable. Science says: just pick one and stop looking back.

---

### 6b. Analysis Paralysis -- The Term Itself

First appeared in The Times (UK) in the 1970s. The rhyme "analysis, paralysis" appears together in an 1803 pronouncing dictionary. In chess: when a player is so overwhelmed by possibilities that their turn takes forever, usually in a losing position.

**Why it's gold:** The NAME is the diagnosis. Analysis literally causes paralysis. The more you think, the less you move. Thinking IS the disease.

---

### 6c. The Default Effect -- Organ Donation

When people can't decide, they choose whatever is already selected. Organ donation rates are 90%+ in countries where the DEFAULT is "yes" (opt-out) and ~15% in countries where you have to opt in. Same decision. Different default. Completely different outcome.

**Source:** Johnson & Goldstein (2003), "Do Defaults Save Lives?" *Science* 302.

**Why it's gold:** Humans would rather let a FORM decide whether their organs get donated than make the choice themselves. We'll surrender our KIDNEYS to a checkbox default.

## 7. COMEDY & POP CULTURE INDECISION

### 7a. Seinfeld -- "The Chinese Restaurant" (1991)

George to a payphone hog: "You know, we're living in a SOCIETY! We're supposed to act in a civilized way!"

**Source:** Seinfeld, Season 2, Episode 11, "The Chinese Restaurant" (1991).

### 7b. Monty Python -- The Bridge of Death (1975)

To cross the Bridge of Death, answer three questions. King Arthur gets: "What is the airspeed velocity of an unladen swallow?" Arthur asks: "What do you mean? An African or a European swallow?" The bridgekeeper -- stumped by his own question -- is thrown into the gorge.

**Source:** *Monty Python and the Holy Grail* (1975).

**Why it's gold:** Arthur defeats a life-or-death test by demanding CLARIFICATION. The bridgekeeper dies of his own vagueness. Sometimes the answer to a question is a better question.

### 7c. Yogi Berra -- "When You Come to a Fork in the Road, Take It"

The origin: Berra was giving directions to his house in Montclair, NJ, to his friend Joe Garagiola. The road forked -- and both forks led to Berra's house.

**Source:** Quote Investigator traces the expression to 1913 (as a fork/utensil pun). Berra embraced it by 1998. From his book *When You Come to a Fork in the Road, Take It* (2001).

**Why it's gold:** Accidentally the most profound advice about indecision ever given. Just PICK ONE. It probably doesn't matter. Both roads go to the same house.

### 7d. Oscar Wilde -- "I Can Resist Everything Except Temptation"

> "I can resist everything except temptation."

**Source:** Oscar Wilde, *Lady Windermere's Fan* (1892), spoken by Lord Darlington.

### 7e. Groucho Marx -- "Those Are My Principles"

## 8. FABLES & ANCIENT WISDOM ABOUT INDECISION

### 8a. Aesop -- The Dog and Its Reflection

A dog carrying a bone sees its reflection in a stream. Thinking it's another dog with a BETTER bone, it opens its mouth to grab the other bone -- and drops its own into the water. Loses both.

**Source:** Aesop's Fables (c. 600 BCE). Also known as "The Dog and the Shadow."

**Why it's gold:** Wanted what it already had, but shinier. Lost the real thing chasing the reflection. Every dating app user in history.

---

### 8b. Dante's Vestibule of Hell (Inferno, Canto 3)

In Dante's Hell, the souls who never committed to anything -- the "neutrals" -- are in the VESTIBULE. Not even worthy of Hell proper. They chase a blank banner for eternity while wasps sting them.

Popularized (inaccurately) by JFK in 1956: "The hottest places in hell are reserved for those who, in a period of moral crisis, maintain their neutrality."

**Source:** Dante Alighieri, *Inferno* (c. 1320), Canto 3. The JFK paraphrase appeared in 1944 (Henry Powell Spring, *What Is Truth*) before JFK used it. The "hottest places" bit is NOT actually in Dante -- in his Hell, the neutrals are in a relatively mild but humiliating area.

**Why it's gold:** Dante's worst punishment for the indecisive: you don't even get to be in Hell. You're in the LOBBY. You're chasing a blank flag -- a banner with nothing on it, because you never stood for anything. Even Hell won't claim you.

## 9. INTERNET & MODERN INDECISION

### 9a. The AITA Pattern

The funniest AITA posts follow two patterns:

**Pattern 1 -- The Innocent Title:** "AITA for giving my stepdaughter a delicious apple?" Body: "Yes, the apple was poison and she now lives as a maid for 7 dwarves."

**Pattern 2 -- The Person Who Clearly Knows:** Posts where the title says "Am I overthinking this?" and the body contains 3,000 words of overthinking. They didn't need Reddit. They needed to put down the phone.

**Source:** r/AmITheAsshole observations, aggregated by BuzzFeed, Bored Panda, etc.

**Why it's gold:** AITA is a temple of indecision. Millions of people asking strangers to make decisions they've already made. The question isn't "am I the asshole?" -- the question is "please tell me I'm not."

---

### 9b. The 3 AM Replay Loop

Common themes from internet culture:
- "Me at 3 AM wondering if I was rude to someone in 2014"
- "Can't sleep because I'm thinking about that weird thing I said at a party 6 years ago"
- Replaying conversations and revising what you SHOULD have said
- Googling your own symptoms until WebMD says you're dying
- Reading the same paragraph 4 times because your brain is running a different program

**Why it's gold:** The internet has revealed that EVERYONE replays decisions at 3 AM. It's not a personal failing. It's a shared species-wide condition.

---

### 9c. The Domino's Tattoo Incident (2018)

Domino's Russia offered 100 years of free pizza to anyone who got the Domino's logo tattooed on their body. Expected a handful. 350 people showed up WITH FRESH TATTOOS within days. Domino's panicked and ended the promotion after one week.

**Source:** CNBC, Fortune, Fast Company, 2018.

**Why it's gold:** 350 people made a permanent body decision for temporary pizza. The fastest commitment to a choice in human history -- and the company was the one that panicked.
