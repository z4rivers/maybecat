## RAW QUOTES — The People Who Are Dead Serious

### The Berkeley Scientists (2017, Proceedings of the Royal Society A)

Paper title: "The roles of impact and inertia in the failure of a shoelace knot"

**Oliver O'Reilly**, Berkeley professor of mechanical engineering:
> "It seemed like a great mechanics problem and no one had solved it."

> "We are trying to understand knots from a mechanics perspective, such as why you can take two strands and connect them in a certain way that can be very strong, but another way of connecting them is very weak."

The study began because O'Reilly's 4-year-old daughter asked him why shoelaces come untied and he couldn't find a good explanation. A professor of mechanical engineering, stumped by a preschooler's shoelace question.
(Source: Berkeley News, Cal Alumni Association — "Knotty Circumstance: How That Shoelace Study Went Viral")

**Christine Gregg**, grad student and co-author:
> "The interesting thing about this mechanism is that your laces can be fine for a really long time, and it's not until you get one little bit of motion to cause loosening that starts this avalanche effect leading to knot failure."

"Avalanche effect leading to knot failure." This is how scientists talk about your shoe coming untied.

**Christopher Daily-Diamond**, grad student and co-author:
> "You really need both the impulsive force at the base of the knot and you need the pulling forces of the free ends and the loops. You can't seem to get knot failure without both."

> "When you talk about knotted structures, if you can start to understand the shoelace, then you can apply it to other things, like DNA or microstructures, that fail under dynamic forces."

They literally compared shoelaces to DNA. SHOELACES TO DNA.

**From the paper's own language:**
- "High-speed video observation of in situ shoelace knot showed failure to be a sudden and catastrophic phenomenon."
- "The failure of the knot happens in a matter of seconds, often without warning, and is catastrophic."
- "Catastrophic" meaning complete, total collapse.
- They describe the forces as "an invisible hand, loosening the knot and then tugging on the free ends."

The academic phrase "cyclic impact loading" is used to describe walking.

(Sources: Royal Society Publishing, Berkeley News, ScienceDaily, Phys.org, PBS News)

### Ian Fieggen — "Professor Shoelace"

An Australian man who has devoted his life to shoelaces. Inventor of the Ian Knot. Runs Ian's Shoelace Site. Has been interviewed by GQ, The Wall Street Journal, Runner's World, ABC Radio, The Washington Post.

> "To me, it's truly fascinating that something as simple as a shoelace could be customized by so many different people in so many different ways for so many different applications."

> "I'm really not a knotting nut."

(He is. He absolutely is.)

His origin story: In 1982, one of his shoelaces broke on the right end. He noticed it was ALWAYS the right end that broke. In the process of trying to make the knot symmetrical, he accidentally stumbled upon a much faster way of tying. This became the Ian Knot.

A Peace magazine interviewer said to him: "Lacing shoes seems like a bit of an obsession with you."

He calculated there are approximately 2 TRILLION possible ways to lace a shoe with 12 eyelets: 1,961,990,553,600 combinations. Almost 2 trillion possibilities every time you put on a shoe and you just do crisscross like some kind of animal.

His site represents "a different time on the internet, when people simply made websites that they were passionate about."

(Sources: Ian's Shoelace Site, Syntopikon interview, Keikari.com interview, Aftermath)

### Burkard Polster — The Mathematician Who Wrote THE BOOK

Authored "The Shoelace Book: A Mathematical Guide to the Best (and Worst) Ways to Lace Your Shoes" — published by the American Mathematical Society.

His findings: There are 43,200 functional ways to lace a shoe with twelve eyelets. The shortest lacing is "bowtie lacing" (rarely used). But the traditional favourite lacings are still the strongest.

The efficient lacing problem is mathematically a special case of the bipartite travelling salesman problem (BTSP). He published the original findings in Nature. The story was picked up by National Geographic and the New York Times.

A man got published in Nature — NATURE — for shoelaces.

(Sources: American Mathematical Society, Plus Maths, ResearchGate)

## FACTS THAT SOUND MADE UP BUT ARE REAL

### The Physics
- When running, your foot strikes the ground at **seven times the force of gravity**. Your shoelace knot absorbs 7g of force with every step.
- Knot failure is described scientifically as "sudden and catastrophic" — happening in as few as **two strides** after the loosening begins.
- There are two ways to tie a standard shoelace bow. One is based on a **square knot** (strong). One is based on a **false knot** (weak). Most people have no idea which one they tie.
- The strong version: two lace crossings of **opposite handedness**. The weak version: two lace crossings of the **same handedness**, causing the knot to twist instead of lying flat.

### The History
- **Harvey Kennedy** patented the modern shoelace with aglet in **1790** and allegedly earned **$2.5 million** from it. In 1790 money.
- The **oldest shoe ever found** is the Areni-1 shoe from Armenia, dating to **3500 BC** — 5,500 years old. It features leather lacing through **15 eyelets**. The shoelaces survived. Five and a half thousand years and the laces are still there.
- Romans made aglets from **gold, silver, brass, and precious stones**. Aglets were STATUS SYMBOLS before buttons existed.
- In the **Medieval era**, aglets were "intricately engraved or shaped" — essentially jewelry for your string.
- The word "aglet" comes from Old French "aguillette" (little needle), from Latin "acucula" (ornamental pin), from "acus" (needle). Your shoelace tip has a Latin pedigree.

### The Mathematics
- There are approximately **1,961,990,553,600** ways to lace a shoe with 12 eyelets. Almost 2 trillion.
- There are **43,200 functional** ways to lace a standard shoe.
- The shortest lacing method is called **"bowtie lacing"** — almost nobody uses it.
- The efficient lacing problem is a special case of the **bipartite travelling salesman problem**.
- A mathematician published a paper about shoelace lacing in **Nature** — one of the most prestigious scientific journals on earth.

### The Records
- **Fastest time to lace a shoe:** 15.51 seconds (Alvaro Martin Mendieta, Spain, 2020)
- **Fastest time to lace a PAIR:** 31.96 seconds (same guy)
- **Fastest with ONE HAND:** 25.49 seconds (SAME GUY)
- **Fastest to tie 3 pairs in a bow:** 9.30 seconds (STILL THE SAME GUY, 2024)
- **Most people tying shoelaces simultaneously:** 467 (Singapore, 2018)
- Alvaro Martin Mendieta is the undisputed GOAT of competitive shoelace athletics.

### The Term "Shoestring Budget"
In the 18th century, "shoestring" referred to a small amount of money. When someone couldn't afford new shoes, they'd repair old ones with simple string. Hence "shoestring budget." The cheapest part of the cheapest thing became a metaphor for poverty.

## THE VOCABULARY — Jargon Gold Mine

### Lace Parts
- **Aglet** — the tip (plastic or metal sheath)
- **Eyelet** — the hole the lace goes through
- **Grommet** — the metal ring reinforcing an eyelet
- **Throat** — the opening of the shoe where laces cross
- **Vamp** — the front panel the laces sit on
- **Quarter** — the rear panels flanking the laces
- **Tongue** — the strip under the laces protecting the foot

### Lacing Methods (Selected — there are hundreds)
- **Crisscross** — the basic one everyone does
- **Straight European** — clean horizontal bars on top, diagonal underneath
- **Straight Bar** — parallel bars, very formal
- **Ladder Lacing** — horizontal + vertical weave, used by US paratroopers
- **Spider Web** — decorative weave, sometimes on military boots
- **Display Lacing** — how shoes are laced in stores (designed for easy try-on)
- **Bow Tie** — vertical inside, crossovers forming a bow tie shape
- **Railroad** — doubled-up passes that look like train tracks and sleepers
- **Loop Back** — each side loops back on itself, like intertwined springs
- **Starburst** — all segments cross diagonally at the middle
- **Black Hole** — laces cross diagonally over two focal points
- **Escher Lacing** — resembles M.C. Escher's fish swimming alternately
- **Supernova** — two starbursts stacked on top of each other
- **Pentagram** — exactly what it sounds like
- **Bowtie** — the mathematically shortest lacing (nobody uses it)

### Knot Types
- **Ian Knot** — world's fastest shoelace knot
- **Square Knot** — the "strong" version of the standard bow
- **False Knot / Granny Knot** — the "weak" version (twists instead of lying flat)
- **Secure Knot** — double-loop variant, life-changing according to the internet

### Academic Terminology (actual language used in papers)
- **Knot failure** — your shoelace came untied
- **Catastrophic failure** — your shoelace really came untied
- **Cyclic impact loading** — walking
- **Impulsive force at the base** — your foot hitting the ground
- **Inertial effects of the swinging laces** — your laces flopping around
- **Lace crossings of opposite handedness** — tying your shoe correctly
- **Lace crossings of same handedness** — tying your shoe wrong
- **Dynamic forces** — moving
- **Structural integrity of the knot** — whether your shoe stays tied
