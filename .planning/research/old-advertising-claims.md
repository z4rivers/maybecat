# Old Advertising Claims & Slogans Research

Research into vintage advertising claims from products that no longer exist or whose claims are now known to be wildly inaccurate. Organized by category with exact slogans, psychological tactics, and why they're absurd by modern standards.

---

## CIGARETTES & TOBACCO

### 1. Camel — "More Doctors Smoke Camels Than Any Other Cigarette" (1946-1952)
- **The claim:** Doctors — the people who are supposed to keep you alive — personally prefer Camels.
- **Psychological lever:** Authority bias. If doctors smoke it, it must be safe. Weaponized trust in medical professionals.
- **The trick:** R.J. Reynolds gave doctors free cartons of Camels at medical conventions, then surveyed them asking what brand they smoked. Shocking no one, they said Camels.
- **Why it's absurd:** Literally used bribery to manufacture a statistic, then used that statistic to sell cancer.

### 2. Lucky Strike — "20,679 Physicians Say 'LUCKIES Are Less Irritating'" (1930)
- **The claim:** A precise number of real doctors confirmed Lucky Strikes are gentle on throats.
- **Psychological lever:** Social proof + false precision. The weirdly specific number (not "20,000" but "20,679") makes it feel scientific.
- **The trick:** American Tobacco's ad agency mailed doctors free cartons of Lucky Strikes with a letter asking if they were "less irritating to sensitive and tender throats," while noting "a good many people" had already said yes. Leading question + free product = desired answer.
- **Why it's absurd:** "Less irritating" cigarettes is like "less stabby" knives.

### 3. Lucky Strike — "Reach for a Lucky Instead of a Sweet" (1928)
- **The claim:** Smoking cigarettes is a healthier alternative to eating candy. A DIET aid.
- **Psychological lever:** Vanity and weight anxiety. Targeted women specifically.
- **Why it's absurd:** The American confectionery industry was so furious they launched counter-ads. Meanwhile, Lucky Strike was marketing lung cancer as a weight loss program.

### 4. Lucky Strike — "It's Toasted" (1917 onward)
- **The claim:** Lucky Strike's tobacco underwent a special "toasting" process that made it superior.
- **Psychological lever:** The illusion of a unique process. Made consumers feel they were getting something special and scientific.
- **Why it's absurd:** ALL tobacco is heat-cured. The "toasting" process was not meaningfully different from any other brand's curing. It's like marketing water as "hydration-enhanced."

### 5. Old Gold — "Not a Cough in a Carload" (1926-1934)
- **The claim:** You could smoke an entire shipping container of Old Golds without coughing once.
- **Psychological lever:** Direct rebuttal of the one thing everyone already knew about cigarettes — they make you cough. Denial as marketing strategy.
- **Why it's absurd:** A carload of cigarettes would contain roughly 10 million cigarettes. The claim is that you could smoke ALL of them without coughing.

### 6. Camel — "Not One Single Case of Throat Irritation Due to Smoking Camels!" (1949)
- **The claim:** In a study of Camel smokers, zero throat irritation was found. Zero.
- **Psychological lever:** Absolute certainty. Not "less" irritation — NO irritation. Bold lies are more believable than cautious ones.
- **Why it's absurd:** Inhaling burning plant matter into your throat causes irritation. This is not debatable. This is physics and biology.

### 7. Philip Morris — "Smoking's More Fun When You're Not Worried by Throat Irritation or Smoker's Cough"
- **The claim:** Philip Morris had found a way to eliminate the negative effects of smoking while preserving the fun.
- **Psychological lever:** Permission-giving. Acknowledged the worry (so you feel heard), then dismissed it (so you feel relieved).
- **Why it's absurd:** The fix for "smoker's cough" was apparently not "stop smoking" but "buy our brand."

### 8. Tipalet — "Blow in Her Face and She'll Follow You Anywhere" (1969)
- **The claim:** Blowing cigarette smoke in a woman's face is a seduction technique.
- **Psychological lever:** Sexual promise. If you buy these cigars, women will be attracted to you. Classic desire exploitation.
- **Why it's absurd:** This is assault. Also, the idea that a face full of smoke is an aphrodisiac is maybe the single worst dating advice ever commercially distributed.

### 9. Kent — "The Micronite Filter" (1952-1956)
- **The claim:** Kent's revolutionary Micronite filter was "so safe, so effective it has been selected to help filter the air in hospital operating rooms" and used "to purify the air in atomic energy plants."
- **Psychological lever:** Scientific authority. If hospitals and nuclear plants trust this material, it must be the pinnacle of filtration technology.
- **Why it's absurd:** The Micronite filter was 15-25% crocidolite asbestos. They were filtering your smoke through a known carcinogen. Adding cancer to your cancer.

### 10. Kool Cigarettes — "Put Your Stethoscope on a Pack of Kools and Listen" (1943)
- **The claim:** If you're a doctor, you should literally examine these cigarettes with medical instruments. They'll pass inspection.
- **Psychological lever:** Challenge framing — daring medical professionals to disprove it. Also, menthol = cooling = healing in the consumer's mind.
- **Why it's absurd:** A stethoscope on a pack of cigarettes would tell you nothing. This is like saying "put a thermometer on this cheeseburger."

## PATENT MEDICINES & SNAKE OIL

### 11. Tutt's Pills — "The Greatest Medical Triumph of the Age... One Dose Effects Such a Change of Feeling as to Astonish the Sufferer" (1886)
- **The claim:** A single pill creates a transformation so dramatic it will shock you.
- **Psychological lever:** Desperation + miracle promise. People suffering from real ailments with no real medicine will try anything.
- **Why it's absurd:** If one pill could astonish a sufferer, we wouldn't need hospitals. We'd need pharmacies and nothing else.

### 12. Dr. Williams' Pink Pills for Pale People (1890s-1920s)
- **The claim:** Cured chorea (St. Vitus' Dance), locomotor ataxia, partial paralysis, sciatica, neuralgia, rheumatism, nervous headache, influenza, palpitation of the heart, "lack of ambition," memory loss, impotence, hysteria, "depression of spirits," and "all forms of Female Weakness."
- **Psychological lever:** Cast the widest possible net. Whatever's wrong with you, these pills fix it. Also, the name itself — "Pale People" — targeted insecurity about appearance.
- **The trick:** The pills were mostly ferrous sulfate (iron supplement). They might have actually helped the anemic, which created enough real testimonials to fuel the rest of the lies.
- **Why it's absurd:** "Lack of ambition" and "Female Weakness" as medical diagnoses. Also: one product cannot cure paralysis AND bad vibes.

### 13. Cupidene — "Quickly Cure You of All Nervous Diseases of the Generative Organs, Such as Lost Manhood, Insomnia... Unfitness to Marry" (1890s)
- **The claim:** This pill restores your manhood and makes you marriage-worthy again.
- **Psychological lever:** Sexual shame and masculine anxiety. "Unfitness to Marry" is devastating language — it implies you're defective as a partner.
- **Why it's absurd:** "Unfitness to Marry" as a medical condition treatable by pill. The original Viagra, except it was probably mostly alcohol.

### 14. Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup (1849-1930s)
- **The claim:** Soothes teething babies and restless children. Happy babies on the label. Peaceful nights for parents.
- **Psychological lever:** Desperate, sleep-deprived parents. Targeted the universal agony of a screaming baby at 3 AM.
- **Ingredients:** Morphine and alcohol. For babies.
- **Why it's absurd:** The American Medical Association denounced it after linking it to infant deaths. It "soothed" babies into unconsciousness — or worse. The happy baby on the label was, essentially, a drugged baby.

### 15. Hadacol — "Don't Be Satisfied With Symptomatic Relief!" (1950s)
- **The claim:** A tonic that was "an Aid to Nature in rebuilding the Pep, Strength and Energy of Buoyant Health." Label promised to cure arthritis, asthma, diabetes, epilepsy, heart conditions, gallstones, and tuberculosis.
- **Psychological lever:** Positioned existing medicine as inadequate. YOU deserve more than just treating symptoms — you deserve a CURE.
- **The trick:** Created by Louisiana state senator Dudley LeBlanc, who had zero medical training. The main active ingredient was 12% alcohol — basically fortified wine with B vitamins. People felt great because they were mildly drunk.
- **Why it's absurd:** When asked on a TV show if Hadacol was good for anything, LeBlanc reportedly replied: "It was good for five and a half million dollars for me last year."

### 16. Clark Stanley's Snake Oil Liniment (1890s)
- **The claim:** A cure-all painkiller derived from rattlesnake fat, based on secret knowledge from Hopi healers.
- **Psychological lever:** Exotic mystique + noble savage myth. The "ancient wisdom" angle made it feel like forbidden knowledge.
- **The trick:** When the FDA finally tested it in 1917, it contained mineral oil, red pepper, turpentine, and camphor. Zero snake of any kind.
- **Why it's absurd:** The product that literally gave us the term "snake oil salesman" didn't even contain snake oil.

## RADIOACTIVE PRODUCTS

### 17. Radithor — "A Cure for the Living Dead" (1920s)
- **The claim:** Radium-infused water cures fatigue, impotence, and mental illness. Makes you feel alive again.
- **Psychological lever:** Reclaiming vitality. "Living Dead" is powerful language — if you're exhausted or impotent, you're already dead. This brings you back.
- **Most famous victim:** Wealthy socialite Eben Byers drank 1,400 bottles. His jaw fell off. Then the rest of his skull started disintegrating. Holes formed in his bones. He died in 1932.
- **Why it's absurd:** The Wall Street Journal headline on his death: "The Radium Water Worked Fine Until His Jaw Came Off." The product literally dissolved its most loyal customer.

### 18. Undark Radium Paint — "Made Possible by the Magic of Radium!" (1917-1926)
- **The claim:** Luminous, glow-in-the-dark paint for watches, instrument dials, house numbers. The future is glowing!
- **Psychological lever:** Novelty and wonder. Radium was sci-fi technology made real. Consumers wanted a piece of the future.
- **What happened:** The "Radium Girls" — factory workers who painted watch dials — were told the paint was harmless and instructed to lick their brushes to make fine points. Their jaws rotted. Their bones crumbled. Some literally glowed in the dark before they died.
- **Why it's absurd:** "Magic" was doing a LOT of heavy lifting in that slogan.

### 19. Tho-Radia Beauty Cream — "Science Has Created Tho-Radia in Order to Beautify Women" (1933, France)
- **The claim:** Radium-infused face cream eliminates wrinkles. Science says so.
- **Psychological lever:** Women's vanity + scientific authority. The brand invoked the Curie name (claiming association with "Dr. Alfred Curie" — no relation to Marie) to add legitimacy.
- **Why it's absurd:** Putting radioactive material on your face to look younger. The cream contained radium bromide. Long-term use would have the opposite of the intended effect.

### 20. Doramad Radioactive Toothpaste — "Keep Your Health with Radium Radiation" (1920s-1940s, Germany)
- **The claim:** "Its radioactive radiation increases the defenses of teeth and gums. The cells are loaded with new life energy, the bacteria are hindered in their destroying effect."
- **Psychological lever:** Germ anxiety + science worship. Framed radiation as a defense system — your teeth as a fortress powered by atomic energy.
- **Why it's absurd:** "The cells are loaded with new life energy" is doing unspeakable work. The cells were being loaded with something, but it wasn't life energy.

### 21. Revigator Water Crock (1920s-1930s)
- **The claim:** A ceramic water crock lined with radium ore. You'd fill it with water at night, and by morning the water would be infused with radon gas — marketed as health-giving "invigorated" water. "Drink Vigor! Feel Vigor!"
- **Psychological lever:** Daily ritual + health prevention. Like a Brita filter, but instead of removing impurities, it added radiation.
- **Why it's absurd:** People were voluntarily adding a radioactive gas to their drinking water every single day. The Revigator came with instructions recommending 6+ glasses daily.

## DDT & HOUSEHOLD CHEMICALS

### 22. Pennsalt DDT — "DDT Is Good for Me-e-e!" (1947)
- **The claim:** A singing jingle featuring happy farm animals, a dog, and an apple all celebrating DDT. The ad stated: "The great expectations held for DDT have been realized."
- **Psychological lever:** Cheerful normalization. When cartoon animals sing about a chemical, it feels as safe as a Disney movie.
- **Why it's absurd:** DDT nearly wiped out bald eagles, causes cancer, persists in the environment for decades, and was banned in 1972. But sure, the cow was really happy about it.

### 23. Penn Salt DDT Ad — "Helps Make Healthier, More Comfortable Homes" (1947)
- **The claim:** Featured a mother and infant with DDT being sprayed in home settings — kitchen, nursery, everywhere.
- **Psychological lever:** Maternal protection instinct. A clean home = a safe baby. DDT = ultimate cleanliness.
- **Why it's absurd:** They showed DDT being sprayed around INFANTS. The EPA would later classify it as a probable human carcinogen.

### 24. "Pure White Fireproof Snow" — Asbestos Christmas Decorations (1930s-1950s)
- **The claim:** Fluffy white asbestos marketed as fake snow for Christmas trees. "Pure White" and "Fire Proof" — safety and aesthetics in one product.
- **Psychological lever:** Holiday cheer + fire safety. Your tree won't burn down AND it'll look beautiful. Win-win for the family.
- **Why it's absurd:** Families lovingly sprinkled a known carcinogen on their Christmas trees. Children playing under the tree inhaled asbestos fibers. Ho ho ho.

## FOOD & BEVERAGES

### 25. Sugar Information Inc. — "Note to Mothers" Campaign (1960s)
- **The claim:** A 1964 Time magazine ad told mothers that drinks without sugar wouldn't provide children with the necessary energy. "Sugar... it's a sensible approach to weight control."
- **Psychological lever:** Maternal guilt. If your kids don't have energy, it's because you're depriving them of sugar. Also, sugar helps you LOSE weight — not gain it.
- **The trick:** The Sugar Research Foundation funded studies to shift blame for heart disease from sugar to fat. This deception shaped dietary guidelines for decades.
- **Why it's absurd:** The sugar industry literally told people sugar was a diet food. The FTC shut down the campaign in 1971.

### 26. 7-Up Baby Ads — "Nothing Does It Like Seven-Up!" (1950s)
- **The claim:** Print ads showed babies and toddlers drinking 7-Up, suggesting mothers should give soda to their infants. "This is a wholesome drink" for the youngest family members.
- **Psychological lever:** Wholesome family values. If babies can drink it, it must be pure and good. Also normalized early brand loyalty.
- **Why it's absurd:** Feeding carbonated sugar water to infants. The ads literally showed babies holding bottles of soda. Modern pediatricians would lose their minds.

### 27. Coca-Cola — "The Temperance Drink: A Valuable Brain Tonic and Cure for All Nervous Affections" (1886)
- **The claim:** Coca-Cola was medicine. A brain tonic. A cure for nervous disorders. Also a temperance drink (implying it was a virtuous alternative to alcohol).
- **Psychological lever:** Moral superiority + medical benefit. You're not just having a soda — you're healing your brain while being a good citizen.
- **Ingredients:** Until 1903, Coca-Cola contained approximately 9 milligrams of cocaine per glass.
- **Why it's absurd:** "Brain tonic" is surprisingly accurate when the drink contains cocaine.

### 28. Vin Mariani Cocaine Wine — "Quickly Restore Health, Strength, Energy and Vitality" (1860s-1910s)
- **The claim:** Bordeaux wine infused with coca leaves. Endorsed by Pope Leo XIII (who awarded creator Angelo Mariani a Vatican gold medal), Thomas Edison, Ulysses S. Grant, and Queen Victoria.
- **Psychological lever:** Elite endorsement. When the Pope drinks it, you don't ask questions.
- **Why it's absurd:** The most powerful people in the world were publicly endorsing cocaine wine. The Pope carried a personal flask of it. A Vatican gold medal. For cocaine.

### 29. Vitamin Donuts — "For Pep and Vigor!" (1941)
- **The claim:** Donuts fortified with Vitamin B1. Presented as a health food that would boost Americans' energy.
- **Psychological lever:** Patriotic health during wartime + indulgence without guilt. Have your donut AND your vitamins.
- **Why it's absurd:** The War Food Administration told the Doughnut Corporation of America to stop. Only the flour was enriched, not the donut itself. They suggested the name "Enriched Flour Donuts" instead. The company chose death over that name.

### 30. Fleischmann's Yeast — "Yeast for Health" (1920s-1930s)
- **The claim:** Eating 2-3 cakes of raw yeast per day (dissolved in water or tomato juice) cured constipation, acne, boils, bad breath, and "intestinal fatigue" — a condition invented by Fleischmann's ad agency.
- **Psychological lever:** Disgust/shame about bodily functions + invented ailment. If you feel sluggish, you have "intestinal fatigue" and only yeast can fix it.
- **The trick:** Sales tripled between 1917 and 1924 after hiring the J. Walter Thompson agency. Consumer Reports disputed the claims in 1936.
- **Why it's absurd:** They invented a disease ("intestinal fatigue"), blamed modern civilization for causing it, then sold the cure — which was raw baking yeast in a glass of water.

## BEAUTY & HYGIENE

### 31. Listerine — "Often a Bridesmaid, Never a Bride" (1924)
- **The claim:** "Edna's case was really a pathetic one... Like every woman, her primary ambition was to marry." Edna couldn't get married because of her halitosis (bad breath). Listerine was the fix.
- **Psychological lever:** Fear of social rejection + shame about your body. If you're single, it's not bad luck — it's your breath. Also weaponized the word "halitosis" — Listerine didn't invent bad breath, but they medicalized it.
- **The trick:** "Halitosis" was an obscure medical term Listerine resurrected specifically to make bad breath sound like a disease you needed to treat. Their campaign is credited with mainstreaming the term.
- **Why it's absurd:** Listerine was originally marketed as a surgical antiseptic and a floor cleaner. They pivoted to mouthwash by convincing people their breath was destroying their love lives. Revenue went from $115,000 to $8 million in seven years.

### 32. Lysol — "For Complete Feminine Hygiene, Rely on Lysol" (1920s-1960s)
- **The claim:** Women should use Lysol — a household disinfectant — as a vaginal douche for "feminine hygiene."
- **Psychological lever:** Marital anxiety + shame. Ads showed unhappy couples and suggested the woman's "neglect" of feminine hygiene was driving her husband away. Sub-text: birth control (since contraception couldn't be advertised directly).
- **Why it's absurd:** Women were douching with FLOOR CLEANER. Lysol in this era contained cresol, which caused inflammation, burning, and even death in some cases. Historian Andrea Tone notes it was actually the best-selling method of contraception during the Great Depression. It was also wildly ineffective as contraception.

### 33. Tho-Radia Lipstick & Powder — "Contains Real Radium" (1930s)
- **The claim:** Lipstick and face powder containing radium and thorium. Applied directly to the face and lips, daily.
- **Psychological lever:** Science + beauty. Radium was associated with energy and vitality. If it powers machines, imagine what it does for your skin.
- **Why it's absurd:** Women were applying radioactive material to their lips. THEIR LIPS. The organ they eat with, kiss with, lick when nervous.

## BABY & CHILDREN'S PRODUCTS

### 34. Bayer Heroin — Cough Suppressant for Children (1898-1910)
- **The claim:** Heroin marketed alongside Aspirin as a cough remedy. Recommended for children. Mixed with sugar and spices to mask the bitter taste.
- **Psychological lever:** Brand trust (Bayer was already respected) + parental concern about children's coughs and pain.
- **Why it's absurd:** Bayer literally invented branded heroin. The name "Heroin" was trademarked by Bayer — derived from "heroisch" (heroic in German) because of how it made patients feel. They gave heroin to children for coughs.

### 35. Iver Johnson Revolvers — "Papa Says It Won't Hurt Us" (1904)
- **The claim:** Featured a little girl holding a revolver, with the tagline assuring that the gun was "perfectly safe." The ad insisted "Accidental Discharge Impossible."
- **Psychological lever:** Safety anxiety flipped into sales tool. Parents worried about guns in the home? Don't worry — this one is child-safe!
- **Why it's absurd:** An ad showing a child holding a loaded revolver with the promise that "Papa says it won't hurt us." The transfer bar safety was innovative for its time, but marketing guns as toys for children is peak Gilded Age insanity.

### 36. Shoe-Fitting Fluoroscope — "It's Dangerous to Have Your Child's Shoes Fitted Any Other Way" (1930s-1950s)
- **The claim:** Shoe stores featured X-ray machines where children stuck their feet inside to see their bones, ensuring "perfect fit." One ad claimed that NOT using the fluoroscope was actually dangerous.
- **Psychological lever:** Parental duty + technology worship. A responsible parent uses SCIENCE to fit shoes. Anything less is negligence.
- **Why it's absurd:** Children were being repeatedly X-rayed at shoe stores. Sales clerks operating the machines had no training. Some children visited multiple times for fun. At peak popularity, there were 10,000 fluoroscopes in American shoe stores.

### 37. Thorazine — For "Hyperactive" Children and "Agitated" Elderly (1950s-1960s)
- **The claim:** Advertisements showed happy, calm children and peaceful elderly patients. "Thorazine... for the agitated, belligerent senile." Also marketed for children's "hyperactivity," hostility, sleep problems, and even vomiting.
- **Psychological lever:** Caregiver exhaustion. Your difficult child or agitated elderly parent can be chemically managed into compliance.
- **Why it's absurd:** Thorazine is a heavy-duty antipsychotic. Using it to calm "difficult" children and sedate old people who were inconvenient. The ads essentially said: "Is someone annoying you? Drug them."

## DIET & WEIGHT LOSS

### 38. "Sanitized Tape Worms — Jar Packed" (Early 1900s)
- **The claim:** "Eat! Eat! Eat! And Always Stay Thin!" Swallow a sanitized tapeworm. It lives in your gut and eats your calories for you. "No diet, no baths, no exercise. FAT — the enemy that is shortening your life — BANISHED."
- **Psychological lever:** The ultimate cheat code. Every diet promise concentrated into one horrifying pill. No effort required.
- **Why it's absurd:** You are voluntarily introducing a PARASITE into your body. The word "sanitized" is doing heroic work. Tapeworms can grow up to 30 feet, cause organ damage, and kill you. "Jar-packed" implies there's a factory somewhere carefully packing worms into jars.

### 39. Ayds Diet Candy — "Why Take Diet Pills When You Can Enjoy Ayds?" (1940s-1980s)
- **The claim:** An appetite-suppressant candy. "Ayds curbs your appetite — you automatically eat less — lose weight naturally, safely, quickly." Endorsed by Hollywood celebrities.
- **Psychological lever:** Indulgence as dieting. You get to eat CANDY and lose weight. Having your cake and not eating it too.
- **Why it's absurd:** The product itself was fine (mildly effective appetite suppressant), but the name became catastrophically unfortunate when the AIDS epidemic emerged in the 1980s. Sales dropped 50%. Commercials chirping "Lose weight deliciously with Ayds!" became unintentionally horrifying. The brand died.

### 40. Wate-On — "If You Want to Be Popular, You Can't Afford to Be Skinny!" (1950s-1970s)
- **The claim:** A weight-GAIN supplement marketed primarily to women. "Don't let them call you Skinny!" Promised to add attractive curves and make women socially desirable.
- **Psychological lever:** Social rejection + romantic failure. Being thin = being unlovable. The exact inverse of modern diet culture.
- **Why it's absurd:** The complete 180 from today's body standards. Vintage ads warned that being too thin would ruin your love life. Now the same magazines sell the opposite fear. The insecurity is the product; the direction doesn't matter.

### 41. Amphetamine Diet Pills — "Pep Pills" for Housewives (1950s-1960s)
- **The claim:** Smith, Kline & French marketed Benzedrine and other amphetamines as weight loss aids for housewives. Kept you slim AND gave you energy to clean the house.
- **Psychological lever:** The superwoman myth. Be thin, be energetic, keep a perfect home. Speed helps you do it all.
- **Why it's absurd:** They were selling methamphetamine to suburban housewives as a lifestyle product. The Rolling Stones wrote "Mother's Little Helper" about this exact phenomenon. The "pep" was speed. The weight loss was amphetamine addiction.

## QUACK MEDICAL DEVICES

### 42. Dr. Scott's Electric Flesh Brush — "Cures Rheumatism, Sciatica, Gout, Nervous Debility, Lumbago, Neuralgia, Toothache, and Malarial Lameness" (1880s)
- **The claim:** A hairbrush with magnetized iron rods embedded in the handle. Brushing your body with it would cure basically everything.
- **Psychological lever:** New technology mystique. Electricity was magic in the 1880s. If electricity powers factories, imagine what it does to your body. (The brush wasn't even electric — it was magnetic.)
- **Why it's absurd:** A hairbrush. For malaria. Dr. George A. Scott had no medical training. He also sold magnetic corsets, insoles, and plasters using the same claims.

### 43. Electropathic Belt — "An Exhilarating Health-Giving Current to the Whole System" (1890s-1920s)
- **The claim:** A belt with tiny electrical/magnetic elements worn around the waist that would cure hernias, impotence, kidney disease, and general debility. Ads featured muscular men, beautiful women, and flying cherubs.
- **Psychological lever:** Masculinity anxiety (targeted men worried about "vigor") + technology promises. Electricity flowing through your loins = restored manhood.
- **Why it's absurd:** Tens of thousands were sold. Listed in the 1906 Sears catalogue. A belt. With magnets. For impotence. And hernias. And flying cherubs were involved somehow.

## DRUGS MARKETED AS MEDICINE

### 44. Bayer Heroin — "The Sedative for Coughs" (1898)
- **The claim:** Heroin marketed as a non-addictive alternative to morphine. Recommended for coughs, pain, diarrhea, pneumonia, and asthma.
- **Psychological lever:** Trust in pharmaceutical innovation. Bayer was a respected name. If they say it's safe, it's safe.
- **Why it's absurd:** A product marketed as a NON-ADDICTIVE alternative to morphine turned out to be more addictive than morphine. The name "Heroin" was a Bayer trademark. They sold it over the counter.

### 45. Valium — "For the Anxiety That Comes from Not Being Able to Cope" (1960s-1970s)
- **The claim:** Ads showed overwhelmed housewives, single women, menopausal teachers. One ad introduced "Jan, single and psychoneurotic, 35" with 15 years of failed relationships. Valium would make you your "true self" again.
- **Psychological lever:** Gendered shame. Your unhappiness isn't systemic — it's chemical, and it's YOUR fault. But a pill can fix you.
- **Why it's absurd:** The pharmaceutical industry pathologized normal female emotions (loneliness, frustration, menopause) and sold the cure. One ad showed a woman whose students "did a double-take" after Valium made her "trim and smartly dressed" again. The drug wasn't treating illness — it was enforcing conformity.

## BEVERAGES WITH WILD CLAIMS

### 46. Pluto Water — "When Nature Won't, PLUTO Will" (1900s-1971)
- **The claim:** Strongly laxative mineral water marketed as "America's Physic." During the 1918 flu pandemic, ads claimed "Pluto Water, America's physic, is influenza's natural foe." Also claimed to cure rheumatism, gout, and general malaise.
- **Psychological lever:** Bodily purity obsession. The idea that constipation was the root of all disease. Also, fear of influenza during a pandemic (sound familiar?).
- **Ingredients:** Sodium and magnesium sulfate (powerful laxatives) plus lithium salts.
- **Why it's absurd:** It took 450 railroad cars to ship a year's output in 1919. America was guzzling laxative water laced with lithium and calling it medicine. Sales halted in 1971 when lithium became a controlled substance.

## DECEPTIVE USE OF AUTHORITY

### 47. Trident — "4 Out of 5 Dentists Surveyed Recommend Sugarless Gum for Their Patients Who Chew Gum" (1976)
- **The claim:** The majority of dental professionals endorse this gum.
- **Psychological lever:** Authority + social proof. Dentists know teeth. If they say it's good, buy it.
- **The trick:** Read the fine print: "...for their patients WHO CHEW GUM." They weren't asked "Is chewing gum good for teeth?" They were asked "IF a patient already chews gum, should it be sugarless?" The answer is obviously yes. The 5th dentist who didn't agree was probably saying "don't chew gum at all."
- **Why it's absurd:** This framing trick became so iconic it spawned the entire "9 out of 10 doctors/dentists" trope. It's not a lie — it's a masterclass in misleading truth.

### 48. The Sugar Research Foundation — Funding Anti-Fat Studies (1960s)
- **The claim:** Not a slogan but a coordinated campaign. The sugar industry funded Harvard researchers to publish studies blaming dietary fat (not sugar) for heart disease.
- **Psychological lever:** Scientific authority. Published in the New England Journal of Medicine. If Harvard says fat is the enemy, who are you to disagree?
- **Why it's absurd:** This deception shaped American dietary guidelines for 50+ years. The low-fat craze of the 1980s-1990s (and the corresponding explosion of sugar in "low-fat" products) traces directly back to bought-and-paid-for science.

## HONORABLE MENTIONS

### 49. Kickapoo Indian Sagwa (1880s-1900s)
- **The claim:** Using the name of the Kickapoo tribe to sell "Indian Sagwa" along with Indian Oil, Buffalo Salve, Worm Killer, and Cough Cure. Claimed to be made from roots known only to Native Americans.
- **Psychological lever:** Exotic "noble savage" mysticism. Ancient wisdom that white medicine can't replicate.
- **Why it's absurd:** The Kickapoo Medicine Company had no connection to the Kickapoo people. The products contained common herbs and alcohol. They hired actual Native Americans to perform in "medicine shows" to sell the illusion.

### 50. Kellogg's Battle Creek Health Builder — "Arousing Sluggish Organs to Action" (1927)
- **The claim:** A vibrating belt machine at John Harvey Kellogg's sanitarium. Promised to alleviate high or low blood pressure and "arouse sluggish organs to action" — particularly the colon.
- **Psychological lever:** Scientific wellness + obsession with "inner cleanliness." Kellogg was fixated on colonic health and made it everyone else's fixation too.
- **Why it's absurd:** A jiggling belt for your colon, prescribed by the same man who invented cornflakes partly because he believed bland food reduced sexual urges. The entire Kellogg wellness empire was built on the idea that your intestines were trying to kill you and only vibration and cereal could save you.

## KEY PATTERNS FOR REFERENCE

**Psychological levers used across all categories:**
1. **Authority hijacking** — Doctors, dentists, scientists used as props (Camels, Lucky Strike, Kent, Trident)
