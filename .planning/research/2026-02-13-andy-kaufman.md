Research compiled 2026-02-13. **Why Kaufman = cat energy:** You literally never knew if he was performing or being real. He'd be sweet (Foreign Man) then terrifying (Tony Clifton). He tested boundaries just to see what would happen. He didn't care if you liked him. He committed 100% to bits that made no sense. The death hoax: the ultimate "maybe."

## I. DIRECT QUOTES FROM ANDY KAUFMAN

### On Not Being a Comedian
Source: Rolling Stone, "Beyond Laughter" by David Hirshey, April 1981

1. "I am not a comic, I have never told a joke. The comedian's promise is that he will go out there and make you laugh with him. My only promise is that I will try to entertain you as best I can."

2. "I never told a joke in my life."

3. "They say, 'Oh wow, Andy Kaufman, he's a really funny guy.' But I'm not trying to be funny. I just want to play with their heads."

4. "I just want real reactions. I want people to laugh from the gut, be sad from the gut — or get angry from the gut."

5. "I can manipulate people's reactions. There are different kinds of laughter. Gut laughter is where you don't have a choice, you've got to laugh. Gut laughter doesn't come from the intellect. And it's much harder for me to evoke now."

### On Reality and Performance

6. "What's real? What's not? That's what I do in my act, test how other people deal with reality."
— Widely attributed; sourced via multiple secondary publications. UNVERIFIED original interview source.

7. "Whenever I play a role, whether it's good or bad, an evil person or nice person, I believe in being a purist and going all the way with the role. If I'm going to be a villainous wrestler, I believe in going all the way with it and not breaking character and not giving away to the audience that I'm playing a role. I believe in playing it straight to the hilt."
— Interview discussing wrestling performances; exact publication UNVERIFIED.

8. "I'm not being paranoid. Every time my manager approached big network executives or even cable, they told him I was too dangerous. They couldn't trust me."
— Rolling Stone, "Beyond Laughter," 1981

### On Entertainment

9. "Pure entertainment is not an egotistical lady singing boring songs onstage for two hours and people in tuxes clapping whether they like it or not. It's the real performers on the street who can hold people's attention and keep them from walking away."

10. "I don't care if it's positive or negative, it's gotta be real."
— Quoted in Thank You Very Much documentary (2025)

### On Meditation and Shyness

11. "I knew I had the potential to entertain, but I was too shy. TM really brought the shyness out of me."
— Discussing Transcendental Meditation, which he practiced 3 hours daily from 1968 until death

### On Wrestling

12. "If I play my cards right, I could bring network wrestling back to TV. Unfortunately, to most people, wrestling is a laughingstock. But fortunately, I'm reaching people who otherwise wouldn't watch it."
— Rolling Stone, 1981

13. (To Jerry Lawler) "I wonder what would happen if you just hauled off and slugged me?"
— Before the Letterman taping, 1982. Source: Lawler's own account in interviews.

### On the Great Gatsby Reading

14. "I'm going to give you culture, whether you want it or not."
— Said to audiences before reading The Great Gatsby aloud, refusing to stop

### On His Album Concept

15. "If people buy a record called Andy and His Grandmother, they think, Oh wow, Andy Kaufman made a record with his grandmother. This must be hilarious. And they show a picture of me and my grandmother on the cover. And then they put it on, and it's just a regular conversation."
— From microcassette recordings (1977-1979), released posthumously on Andy and His Grandmother (Drag City, 2013)

### On Faking His Death

— To Bob Zmuda, captured on microcassette. Heard on Andy and His Grandmother album.

## III. PERFORMANCE DESCRIPTIONS — THE RAW MATERIAL

### The Mighty Mouse Lip-Sync (SNL Premiere, October 11, 1975)

Standing in the middle of a bare stage accompanied by an old record player, Kaufman put on a recording of the Mighty Mouse theme song. He stood perfectly still, silent, doing absolutely nothing — until the chorus arrived: "Here I come to save the day!" He lip-synced that single line with enormous enthusiasm and emotion. Then went completely still again. Waited for the next chorus. Repeated. That was the entire act. The premiere of Saturday Night Live. Nobody knew what was happening.

### The Great Gatsby Reading

Kaufman would announce to comedy club audiences: "I'm going to give you culture, whether you want it or not." He'd begin reading The Great Gatsby aloud in an English accent. At first it seems a bit funny, but he doesn't stop. He proceeds to read the book, hushing people when they laugh. Threatening to start all over again if they interrupted. Audiences that had come for comedy sat in rising fury as he read earnestly, page after page. When the crowd finally revolted, he'd offer them a choice: hear him continue reading, or hear a recording of him reading. They'd choose the recording. He'd play a record — of himself reading The Great Gatsby in a British accent.

### 99 Bottles of Beer on the Wall

Kaufman would wander onstage and begin lamely sing-songing, with tiny, mock-festive hops: "A hundred bottles of beer on the wall, a hunnerd bottles of beer." The audience titters at first, chuckles at ninety-two, worried glances by seventy-seven as they realize he's really going all the way. Groans and face-rubbing, halfhearted boos. The voices change — George C. Scott singing "Sixty-six bottles of beer on the wall," Elvis taking them down to fifty-three. He'd increase the pressure by counting back UP for a few bottles. Then, with only 5 or 6 bottles to go, he'd feign recognition that the audience wasn't enjoying it and leave the stage. The audience would call him back to finish. He made them BEG for the thing they'd been begging him to stop.

### Foreign Man into Elvis

Kaufman would appear as "Foreign Man" — meek, heavy accent, claiming to be from "Caspiar." He'd tell jokes wrong. Do terrible impressions. The audience would grow uncomfortable, pitying. Then without warning, he'd launch into a devastating Elvis impersonation — every word a direct quote from Elvis's actual onstage patter, memorized from an album of outtakes. The audience would erupt. Then he'd snap back to Foreign Man's meek voice: "T'ank you veddy much." The shock of the transformation was the point — he'd shown the audience how completely a performer could manipulate their perceptions.

### The Carnegie Hall Show (April 26, 1979)

The most complete summation of Kaufman's career. Sold-out Carnegie Hall. All his classic routines — Foreign Man, Elvis, conga drums, wrestling women, plus a real male wrestler. His "grandmother" sat in a chair at the side of the stage the whole show. At the end, she removed her mask and revealed herself to be Robin Williams. An elderly woman (Eleanor Cody Gould) had a "heart attack" and "died" on stage. Kaufman reappeared wearing a Native American headdress, performed a dance over her body, and "revived" her.

After the show, he took all 2,800 audience members out for milk and cookies. 24 buses. $20,000 spent. The destination: the cafeteria of the New York School of Printing, where hundreds of baggies of Famous Amos cookies awaited. Then he invited anyone still interested to meet him on the Staten Island Ferry the next morning, where the show continued.

### Tony Clifton on the Taxi Set (1978)

Kaufman had negotiated a contract clause giving Tony Clifton — his abrasive lounge singer alter ego — a guest role on Taxi. On Monday morning, a man arrived in very orange makeup, a wig, and fake moustache, chain-smoking in a blue ruffled tuxedo shirt. Danny DeVito: "And the f---ing door opens, and it comes, this f---ing makeup, pounds of makeup on. He's got two hookers, dressed to the nines." Clifton brought prostitutes to work with him. Declared he'd rewritten the script to include them. Was miserable to work with. Couldn't act. Angered everyone present. Judd Hirsch reportedly flipped out during an on-set showdown. Clifton was eventually escorted off the Paramount lot by security. Kaufman never acknowledged being Clifton. Bob Zmuda also played the role — meaning sometimes even the people closest to Kaufman didn't know who was inside the suit.

### The Fridays TV Incident (February 20, 1981)

On ABC's live sketch comedy show Fridays, Kaufman was in a sketch about couples sneaking away to smoke marijuana. Mid-sketch, he broke character and refused to read his lines: "I can't play stoned." Michael Richards (years before Seinfeld) grabbed the cue cards and threw them down on the table in front of Kaufman. Kaufman splashed water in Richards' face. A brawl broke out on live television. The network cut to commercial as cast and crew rushed in. Home viewers had no idea what was happening.

It was all planned. Only a handful of people knew — Kaufman, Richards, the director, and the actors in the sketch. Kaufman never confirmed it was staged. In an interview with People magazine, he accused ABC of a cover-up and denied it was prearranged.

### The Jerry Lawler Feud (1981-1982)

Kaufman entered the world of Memphis professional wrestling, taunting residents by playing "videos showing residents how to use soap" and proclaiming Memphis "the nation's redneck capital." He explained what soap is. How to properly use toilet paper. He was a natural heel — instigating and drawing heat like a born villain.

Jerry Lawler delivered a piledriver that sent Kaufman to the hospital. He was still wearing a neck brace when both appeared on Late Night with David Letterman, July 28, 1982. The planned script was for them to apologize to each other. Instead, Kaufman whispered to Lawler before taping: "I wonder what would happen if you just hauled off and slugged me?" Lawler stood up and slapped Kaufman out of his chair. When the show returned from commercial, Kaufman was on the other side of the stage, screaming profanities at Lawler.

The entire feud was scripted. This was not publicly disclosed for over a decade. Kaufman and Lawler were friends.

### Getting Voted Off SNL (1982)

SNL ran an unprecedented viewer call-in vote: "Should Andy Kaufman continue to appear on Saturday Night Live?" Two 900 numbers. 195,544 voted to DUMP Andy. 169,186 voted to KEEP Andy. The whole thing was cooked up between Kaufman and producer Dick Ebersol. But Kaufman spent the week leading up to the vote genuinely worried it would go against him. It did. He never appeared on SNL again. He died a year later.

### The Intergender Wrestling Championship (1979-1983)

Kaufman proclaimed himself "Inter-Gender Wrestling Champion of the World." Offered $1,000 to any woman who could pin him. Wrestled more than 400 women between 1979 and 1983. On SNL in 1979, he wrestled Lacoste heiress Mimi Lambert for three straight minutes, pinned her, kicked her, and named himself champion. Extra prize: if a woman pinned him, she'd get to marry him and he'd shave his head. Women's groups were furious. Fans were baffled.

## IV. THE CHILDHOOD ORIGIN

At age 4, in 1953, in his bedroom in Great Neck, Long Island, while other children were outside playing, Andy began daily "TV broadcasts" to an imaginary audience in his wall. He'd pretend a camera was in the basement bedroom wall and put on shows for invisible viewers — any genre: variety, talk shows, dramas. Every day.

The origin: his beloved grandfather died unexpectedly. His parents thought he was too young to understand, so they told him grandfather had gone on a long trip. Andy started performing to the wall. As Bob Zmuda later observed: the seed of a man who wanted to play with reality — to make you think one thing when something else is really happening — came from being lied to about death at age 4.

Before he was even a year old, Andy reached from his crib to work the controls of a nearby phonograph. "He'd stand up in his crib and keep it on all the time, just putting the needle back," his mother remembered. At age 9, he began performing at children's birthday parties. His grandmother took him to Times Square, where he was captivated by the freak show at Hubert's Museum and Flea Circus, and to Madison Square Garden wrestling matches — which inspired him to stage his own in his parents' basement.

## V. THE DEATH AND THE MAYBE

Andy Kaufman died of large-cell carcinoma (lung cancer) on May 16, 1984. He was 35. He was a non-smoker. The diagnosis was a shock.

Before dying, he traveled to the Philippines to see faith healer Jun Roxas — a fraud who performed "psychic surgery" using sleight of hand. The irony: the man who built his career on hoaxes fell for one when it mattered most.

But he'd talked about faking his death for years. To Lynne Margulies, Bob Zmuda, Mimi Lambert, John Moffit (Fridays producer). He told Lambert: "If I do go through with my plan, I will do so by pretending to have cancer." He asked Zmuda on tape: "But won't people hate me when they find out that I'm still alive?"

Michael Kaufman (Andy's brother) found an essay Andy wrote about faking his death, including instructions on meeting him at a specific restaurant on Christmas Eve, 1999. Michael went. Andy didn't show.

In 2013, Michael told the Andy Kaufman Awards audience that Andy was alive, in love, and raising a daughter. A young woman came to the stage. She was actress Alexandra Tatarsky, recruited by Michael to play the part. Another hoax about a hoax.

The Los Angeles County Coroner re-released his death certificate: "Andy Kaufman is dead."

But people still aren't sure. That's the ultimate "maybe."

## VI. THE UNFINISHED LECTURE

Near the end of his life, Kaufman planned a college tour — a series of lectures entitled **"On Creating Reality: The Physics of Human Response."** No known notes exist. Its contents remain as enigmatic as the man himself.

The title alone is a philosophy: Reality is something you CREATE. And you measure it by what it does to people.
